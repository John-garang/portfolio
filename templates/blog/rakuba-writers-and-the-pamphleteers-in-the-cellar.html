Rakuba Writers and the Pamphleteers in the Cellar

John GarangNovember 10, 2026 · 4 min read
johngarang.com

Cultural etiquette and the illusion of the silent obedient patriot have long been used since medieval times as tools of containment.

Today, South Sudan's digital landscape is becoming quite volatile; perhaps or perhaps not a bad thing. I took two modules this semester, "Rhetorics" and "Measuring Public Opinion," which shed some light on this. Misuse or proper use of social media has been a topic of constant debate, yet the archetypal fear of emotional and viral communication existed long before Mark's Meta algorithms. It existed because the democratization of speech has always terrified the elite. Stretching back to classical antiquity, this anxiety is not new. Plato in "La République" argued that poets should be totally banished out of fear that they stirred what he coined as uncontrollable passions. By the Age of Enlightenment, writing became open warfare against absolute monarchy.

Another historical reference is the English Crown's campaign against Irish bards, and this is where history gets interesting.

If you meet an Irish bartender today and call them British or English, which could be a slip of tongue or lack of cultural awareness, they will feel deeply insulted. This is partly due to a dark past of the Tudor state and British colonialism. And part of this memory includes a martial law by the British monarchy that ordered the hunting down and slaughter of Irish bards and rhymers. The bards shared a common trait with today's content creators: they wrote catchy, rhythmic verses, which bypassed traditional press and landed in the hearts of the people, prompting countrywide rebellion. The bards were ordinary people of society, like what critics today refer to as "rakuba writers." The execution of Irish bards was meant to kill the Irish language and control public opinion. By silencing the public, they would silence rebellion in the countryside. The Irish did the opposite. They were forced to adopt English, and they did more: they colonized the language, infusing it with the ancient rhythm, grief, and emotional power of their lost oral traditions (this largely influences contemporary English literature). Today, social media does the exact same thing; punchy Facebook posts spread the same viral passion and messages that monarchies spent centuries trying to suppress.

The Catholic Church shared the same fear, prompting them to ban foundational thinkers like René Descartes, whose thinking cast radical doubts on the ruling class and encouraged the public to trust human reason over blind institutional faith. Similarly, during the Enlightenment and Atlantic Revolutions, governments also weaponized seditious libel laws, punishing anyone who shared an opposing view to the king.

All these historical references mirror the same thing we see today in South Sudan and its diaspora: children of nobodies using mass media to strip authoritarian regimes of their dignity by exposing state propaganda. Without the illusion of absolute dignity, totalitarian regimes fear that public opinion imposes on them the need for accountability.

I mean, if the population loses fear and begins to laugh at or scrutinize its leaders, the spell of absolute authority breaks.

There is a current tension in the country where a term coined "rakuba writers" is used to cast doubt on the credibility of the opinion of ordinary writers, framing them as jealous people who have not achieved much for themselves and are envious of the successful elements of the regime. The historical references also point to a similar concept by the French crown: the "pamphleteer in the cellar." These were poor underground writers, without mass wealth or status.

The European crowns deployed several propaganda theories to counter these pamphleteers. One, they framed them as paid pseudo-intellectuals who, out of financial pursuits, tried to stain the image of the kingdom. Even where no connection existed, they were falsely associated with exiled legitimate royalties or heirs to the throne to create an illusion that their opinions were not substantial, and rather based on gains per impact: what today is claimed as paid writers or writing for algorithms — attention seeking for potential gains on views. Interestingly, Meta does not pay for text, no matter the reach — ironic!

Second, they were seen as peasants seeking political influence and rising through capitalizing on the social crisis facing the populace. Their critique was framed as performance and a moral failing — a lack of respect for "elders" — as the powerful shifted the blame onto the pamphleteer's behavior. A similar sentiment is used in South Sudan, where social commentary is framed as attention seeking or clout chasing.

Of course, social commentary carries many risks, one being potential retaliation by the state and elite. The state also holds immense power to use its proxies for character assassination of writers where physical damage may not reach. And there will always be opposition from the ordinary patriot; but these patriots rarely admit they are suppressing truth. Instead, they frame social commentators as moral degenerates who lack the proper "spirit" or cultural etiquette accorded to elders and leaders. It's up to you to choose the line between your values, risks, and other factors.

The two courses are quite interesting, and while the social, political, and economic realities of European medieval times are significantly different from our nation's, they offer a starting point. A starting point to notice the slow but certain rise of youth who are holding up a vital mirror that a changing state needs to survive. Continue sharing these opinions so that the generation that will inherit the state is well informed of the underlying grievances. I am at peace knowing that some of our young people notice that the formation of public opinion or online rhetoric is not bitterness but a vital asset towards political accountability and cultural morality!
