Order of Rope — Part 2

John GarangShort story · April 6, 2026 · 7 min read
johngarang.com

He lived with his mother and little sister in a two-room house that remembered better days. Every morning, he woke before the sun, because sleep felt undeserved. He washed his face slowly, as if afraid to scrub off the little dignity left.

He did not need a wardrobe; he owned only three decent shirts. One white, reserved for Sunday service. One blue, kept for interviews — washed carefully, ironed, worn hopefully, and folded back with disappointment at the end of the day. A black one was worn to funerals, because in these times men attend more burials than celebrations.

Before leaving his room, he would stare at his Political Science degree on the wall, and it would whisper apologies for not translating into bread after all the hurdles of university. Before stepping out, his mother would bless him.

"Today the Lord will open the door," she would say.

"Amen," Deng would answer, the way men do when hope must be rationed.

This short exchange was medicinal; it was what kept the house together.

Outside, the neighbourhood woke differently. Many had lost faith in religion and leaned on fate. They refused to blame misfortune on unseen evil and instead blamed their government. Boys and men of all ages gathered at boda boda stages, kiosks, dusty corners, and shade.

With no real job, Deng spent his mornings walking from office to office, checking job boards. When the sun became unbearable, he took over his friend's rickshaw to earn a few pounds to take home to his old lady. Rickshaws did not move late; the town was now harboured by unruly boys who carried pistols just above their pants. They did not ask twice. Avoiding them was survival.

When evening came, they gathered by the Nile, on a bench where the river slowed. The water moved the way progress should have, but did not. Everyone carried internal struggles, yet when men met, they laughed loudly, argued football, mocked politicians, and spoke of business ideas they would start soon. Deng joined them, chewing a toothpick as if it owed him money.

"Still alive?" Dau would ask.

"Barely," he answered.

Everyone laughed, but Deng's came a second too late — a rehearsed sound, because he had learned not to notice how these conversations felt forced and repeated, like rickshaws in traffic. A man who does not laugh is suspected of softening, and softness here is treated like treason.

One did not have to talk much; they understood each other in the way men do when words cost too much. Each man carried his troubles in his chest. Bul hid accrued debts behind tired humour. Garang, bruises hidden beneath long sleeves, leaned in.

"Heard about the shopkeeper? Pistol boys took his last coins. Now he's a family of three, granary empty."

At the end of the bench, Nun hid a marriage on the brink of collapse beneath unmet expectations. He chuckled bitterly.

"Roads to nowhere. My wife's eyes — cold now. Expects the chief's house from my kiosk dreams."

Deng spat into the water. "Government builds roads we can't fuel."

Beneath the words, he hid fear. Fear that life would be longer than the strength he had to stand again. Fear that education had raised his expectations beyond his reality, making acceptance difficult. Fear of the day his mother would stop saying, "Today the Lord will open the door," and start understanding life the way many Christians the economy had turned into pagans did. Fear that he would become one of those men whose names only travelled when they died. Fear of the day he would befriend the rope.

When silence settled, they bade goodbye, and each man, like a knight, took his road home.

That evening, while eating a meal they could afford, the town women arrived with a name. News spread that a young man had been found hanging on the same tree where another was found a week earlier. They repeated the same sentences in low tones, as if volume could resuscitate him.

They said he was quiet. They said there were no signs it was forced. Deng noticed how words change after death. It surprised him that no one ever asked why — never before, always after.

He did not sleep that night. The house breathed around him as memories of his father's swinging body mixed with echoes of his mother's prayers through the thin walls. They were humble requests offered daily to a God who did not seem bothered by familiar voices.

He sat on the floor, thinking of the hope he carried in college. He murmured about the burdens on his shoulders, counting expectations the way children count fingers. Provider. Protector. Example. Man. No one taught him he could still find peace without fulfilling these words.

The next morning, at the funeral, men stood straight. As custom dictates, grief was managed, collars straightened, faces blank. Women wailed. Deng watched the coffin lower into red soil and wondered how long the man had practised silence before the rope finished the sentence.

He wished he understood the breaking point — the moment one decides to explore beyond what this world offers. The wailing did not bother him; he knew only a thought separated him from the same fate.

That afternoon, when Deng returned, he found his little sister in the living room, legs crossed on the table, books spread across the sofa.

"School fees," she whispered, slowly, as if afraid of hurting words.

She was not manifesting; what choice did she have? The school did not care where money came from. Teachers had to be paid. Deng nodded. Nodding was cheaper than words. He had learned titles do not bring satisfying answers, and acknowledgment sometimes says enough.

That evening, Deng did not go to the river. He waited for his mother to return from the market. After she prepared what little could keep their stomachs warm, he sat with her the way life had not allowed in years.

The prayers and courage holding the house together were no longer heavy enough to hold his soul. His mouth opened before his mind fully agreed to fall apart.

"I am tired," he said.

The house felt smaller. The words rested between his lips and the fragility of being emotional. He waited — for rebuke, culture, reminders — but none came. His mother sat still, eyes sunken in a way he had never truly seen.

"Talk," she said.

He did. Like a security light sensing movement. He spoke of rejection emails with no reasons, of letters thrown away as soon as he left rooms. Of praise ending with, "You are very talented, but we will go with other candidates." Of people asking for help, assuming he had arrived. He spoke of fear — not of lack, but of becoming a quiet man whose silence would only be noticed once.

His mother listened the way old trees listen to the Nile. When he finished, she broke down. She carried her own struggles, perhaps deeper, but today was not about her. This was her boy, wearing shoes too large.

Wiping her tears, she said, "I raised you to live, not to perform. We may lack, but your presence means everything to me. Your father kept to himself, and I blame myself for not seeing beyond his silence. Maybe if he had whispered, we would still have him. Silence breaks families more than lack."

Something loosened. Deng cried quietly — the way men cry when relief arrives. He did not have much, but he learned life was more than what he could provide.

The next morning, he woke early. When he looked at his degree, it seemed to smile back at him. He had not arrived, but he had tools, and that was enough fuel to feed the days of waiting.

When his mother blessed him and said, "Today the Lord will open the door," he replied, "And if He doesn't, it doesn't mean He has forgotten me."

Their tears did not soften the economy or carry a job to their door, but something had shifted; Deng no longer felt the burden of responsibility; he felt purpose. The house no longer stood on hope; it stood on will, and will is the greatest foundation.

That evening, back at the bench, as was routine, Dau asked, "How is life?"

Deng paused — not out of lack of words, but because he no longer wished to keep to himself.

"Hard," he said, and dropped the words there.

No one laughed. Another man exhaled and shared his troubles, and then, like a queue, everyone followed, like knights released from duty. Like pages of a chapbook, topics turned — debt, fear, doubt, waiting, and so on. The bench would not solve them, but it listened. It does not judge them, for worse men have sat here, and silence paid them nothing.

The Tulip tree bloomed, matured, declined, and finally rested. Deng and his friends never became remarkable. They never left Sherkat. But they did not keep quiet; they showed up and complained, because they learned silence does not strengthen men — it erases them, one at a time.

No one knows what became of Deng, as the elders who told these stories laid down their tools and became the ancestors they once honoured. And Sherkat still prayed to be marked on maps.
