The Long Walk to the Library

John GarangNovember 14, 2026 · 3 min read

Last week, I came across a story online about the Kenya National Library Service (KNLS) – Nakuru Branch, and I just don't know why anyone would think I'm showing off when I speak fluent and flawless English. 🥱

Guy… I spent years in a private primary school, and you expect me to speak a half-baked language? But that's not even the thing. I also grew up with very driven cousins. During the holidays, after lunch, we would walk from our house in Section 58, all the way through Kingdom Seekers, to Christ the King Cathedral. I also recall that St. Xavier's Nursery and Pri. School was around that area. The library was located in Biashara Ward, Nakuru Town East.

That was roughly a 1.5-hour journey, and all that was just to go and read books at the public library. And mind you, this was around 2010–2014.

Looking back, those were some of the most important years of our intellectual formation. We were children, but we were already developing habits that would shape how we think, communicate, and understand the world. Those walks were not just about getting to a library; they were about building a relationship with books, curiosity, and knowledge. Some things just need common sense. A child who spends their holidays reading is likely to develop a different relationship with language and information from one whose only exposure to entertainment is a television screen. Schooling is exposure, but genuine education is what truly transforms. Yet we must acknowledge that transformation requires access to information, opportunities to explore, and environments that encourage intellectual curiosity. A public library is such an institution that quietly shapes a child's understanding of the world.

And that is where the contrast becomes interesting. While we were busy doing just that, there was you, watching free musara through a nail-sized window in a privately owned video hall.

But this also makes me think about South Sudan's development status. What a useless government we have! Years after independence, we are still struggling with the most basic institutions that should support intellectual development. We don't even have a functioning public library system that our children can proudly call their own? And no, those little bookstores are not worth calling public libraries; the ones I am talking about are just about the size of some of our universities in Juba. Yet there are people who still worship that old hag called Kiir! Abeg, release me.

This is not another rant on Abudigin; it's about the kind of society we are deliberately or unintentionally creating. What intellectual foundation are we laying for children who have to rely almost entirely on their schools, families, or whatever information happens to find its way onto their phones?

Our government has not only failed to provide roads, hospitals, schools, electricity, or even common sense. It has also failed by neglecting the intellectual foundations of its population, hence the reason we have more fools than we did before independence. Why are we neglecting the essence of creating an environment where our children can develop the capacity to question, understand, imagine, and contribute to society?

And honestly, you cannot keep complaining about the quality of English, public debate, or intellectual engagement during these elections, and do so little to nurture these very capabilities. But anyway, they will be done. I will speak the English that looks like you, but just so you know, I can also speak like that favorite CNN journalist of yours!
