Better-Dressed Rebels With Smartphones

John GarangNovember 9, 2025 · 5 min read
johngarang.com

I have been working professionally in the media and communications field for close to three years. Not too long, but long enough to learn one undeniable truth: words and images can either break or make communities. Media is not a natural entity; it is a weapon or a medicine, depending on how it is used. Yet in South Sudan, we often underestimate the power of what we post, share, or engage with online. The media is not a toy, and if we treat it as such, it will continue to damage our communities.

Our country is learning to walk again — on crutches — fragilely recovering from decades of civil war. But its wounds are still fresh, and this limping toward recovery can quickly turn into another tragedy. We host some of the least educated people in the world, yet with the highest emotions. And in this condition, what we spread online matters more than we realize. Our words aren't merely alphabets, and our photos aren't just colors on paper or screens — they are triggers to bullets, or bandages to the wounds of war. What is shared online is a seed in the minds of our youth. But the sad reality is, too many of the seeds planted by South Sudanese influencers are weeds — and these plantations decide where this country goes next.

The most important aspect of media influence is a large audience, without which whatever is shared online doesn't hold much ground. In our country, influence is measured by followers and likes — not by the lives changed or minds awakened. As Bóth Mawich II put it the other day, isn't it strange — almost painful — that the youth of this country scroll past anything educational, yet stop to argue over political propaganda, appointments, and dismissals? Sadly, most — if not all — of our media professionals lack credible formal training or ethical standards to professionally execute their jobs. They do not understand that media should be an instrument of peace, not the war cry of division.

Most of our influencers take controversy as a career. They are fishermen of an ignorant audience, and our semi-illiterate Junubin, like fish, go for their bait. Influence in Junub has lost its sacred duty of preaching the kind of truth that mends, not tears apart.

However, we are not totally hopeless. You don't have to look far to see how influence should be used. Ajak Deng Chiengkou is the kind of man whose education is not shown by degrees, but in his deeds. Since late 2018, I have closely followed Ajak's work — from his articles to his interviews — and there's something uniquely promising about his education. Ajak uses his pen as a broom to sweep away ignorance and harmful traditional practices. He has also been a steward of our culture, leaving behind what slay queens call "soft life" in Australia to travel to the most remote and inaccessible areas of our country, documenting the beauty of our traditions. Ajak uses his influence to teach what a community should look like, and his work has not gone unnoticed. It is an honor to appreciate what he does. His writing carries the weight and wisdom of our ancestors. He writes as if words can build character — and they do.

But not all pens are positively intentional or clean. Some dip their pens in dirt. Take, for instance, Ateny Wek Ateny. His audience may ignorantly think my criticism is politically motivated, but in all honesty, Ateny's pen teaches the youth political writing with the loyalty of a chameleon. Just yesterday, he was a staunch supporter of the VP of the Economic Cluster — defending the man from all corners of the country as if the VP's shoes walked on gold. It was clear that his intentions were to seek political favor from the man he once described as an ambitious patriot. But now that the VP is cornered and losing political ground, Ateny's wind has shifted. His pen now swings like a sword toward the same man he once guarded with the last of his intellect. The youth watching him are learning that truth is for sale — and that's the sickness killing our politics. Ateny is a living reminder that when politics becomes a business, truth becomes the first casualty.

Next is Kijana Ali, the allegedly overqualified, seven-degree, Australia-based diehard supporter of the SPLM Chairman. Kijana's seven degrees — which should reflect in his command of language — leave you wondering if they were bought at "Ser Akuan" in Konyo Konyo. His writing and speech sometimes struggle to pass primary five. Leveraging his large and gullible audience, Kijana parades himself as a scholar obsessed with the Baba Kiir Mayardit abii door and development gospel — a slogan that's laughable, considering how the old man is sinking this country. Kijana's singing makes one wonder if he hopes his melody might somehow drown the truth. I sometimes wonder if he listens to the lyrics of his own songs.

Kijana also pushes harmful narratives about women and communities. He speaks carelessly, and most of his victims choose silence — not out of agreement, but because responding to him would mean dragging oneself down. A conviction Ajak himself learned this year.

Then comes Tearz Ayuen, once a sharp critic, now a soft defender of his political kin. Ayuen, who undeniably commands language and influence, might be educated — but education that serves only one's throat is merely a walk through schools. His stance shifts like canoes in Bor during the floods — floating wherever the current pays more. His pen, too, bleeds tribalism, often aimed at the Nuer, painting one community as the sole carrier of the nation's sins.

Closing the show are Sarah Madecdec, Gokwel, and their TikTok disciples. On second thought, they might be excused — they are mostly illiterate, just lucky to have moved abroad where even the foolish earn what a PhD holder struggles to get back home. This group has turned shame into content. If TikTok's censorship algorithm were ever trained in Dinka, these two wouldn't just be banned — they'd be charged with crimes. They shamelessly trade dignity for views. And because we have a poorly informed population, they continue to attract an audience that keeps rewarding such content. They specialize in body-shaming, propaganda, and stripping culture bare for applause from strangers. In their world, private parts have no privacy. But the greater problem lies with those who laugh along, forgetting that laughter too can destroy a nation's soul.

Our country isn't only politically in turmoil — it's socially a disaster. The seeds we plant in the minds of our youth continue to shape the nation. All the nudes going viral draw inspiration from the influencers we glorify. We are breeding careless words and cheap influence. And the problem is not that we lack voices — it's that too many have nothing worth saying.

I have seen the inside of a newsroom and the noise of social media. And I can say this: if we cannot use our influence to build peace and a morally upright society, then we are just better-dressed rebels with smartphones.
