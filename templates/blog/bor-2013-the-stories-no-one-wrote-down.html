Bor, 2013: The Stories No One Wrote Down

John GarangJune 2, 2026 · 5 min read

Yesterday, I had a 5-hour chat with my girlfriend that started with our own lives and experiences before slowly taking shape around the Bor Massacre of 2013/2014. She was only 10 years old when she had to run for her life, often getting lost in the chaos several times.

On December 18, 2013, at 02:30, around 2,000 soldiers under Peter Gadet, the commander of the 8th Division, a primarily Nuer unit, defected and launched a surprise attack with tanks, rockets, mortars, and artillery on the Panpandiar and Malual-Chaat military camps outside Bor, overrunning them by around 05:00. The city changed hands four times between December 18, 2013, and January 18, 2014, ultimately leaving the SPLA in control.

Official records indicate 2,700 casualties within the one-month conflict, mostly civilians. Comparisons have been made to the Bor Massacre of 1991, during the Second Sudanese Civil War, in which Nuer forces under Riek Machar killed an estimated 2,000 Dinka. (These numbers are nowhere close to the real ones, but I am heading there.)

Victims of the ethnic cleansing in Bor in 2013 will never receive justice, not even the least recognition, as their stories are often treated as a shadow. The firsthand witnesses have failed to document the inhumane activities that no one mentions in records of the 2013/14 civil war.

Now, as someone who was in Kenya at the time, I never knew the severity of the situation. Local news described it as a civil war, something small, just another headline that you read and move on from. One minute you are reading the news, and the next minute you are watching a comedy series and laughing. But back then, I never knew all my siblings were almost at the point of death, and that my mother could have succumbed to a heart attack.

The first real account that showed me how serious this was came in 2018, when I watched a documentary about the ethnic cleansing of Nuer in Juba. It was horrific, nothing like I had ever imagined before.

The second was when I heard the full story from my mother and my siblings. My mother was at her niece's wedding in Juba, together with her brother and his wife. My siblings were left at home with my cousins, the oldest being fourteen years old. My siblings were 10, 8, and 5 years old at the time, while my cousins were 12, 9, and 6, along with another cousin, a 14-year-old girl.

The orders were clear, from the same Riek that people want us to glorify today. 1991 is often given the most attention, but in 1991, the White Army was ordered to kill men and youth, while doing as they wished with women. In 2013, the warlord Riek ordered his forces to kill everyone: children, adults, and the elderly. There was no bargain.

My girlfriend recalls seeing people get shot just behind her. But she was only 11 years old, small and light enough to keep running. She eventually made her way to Biong, where she was reunited with her family before having to flee again to Angakuei.

The stories surrounding my siblings' survival are too long to tell here, but my mother was able to coordinate with her brother and have them evacuated to Guolyar in Mingkaman, then to Juba, and subsequently to Nimule.

To shed light on why I feel none of these stories are documented, my girlfriend told me that while they were in Angakuei, there were not enough boats to carry everyone. You must imagine that some families had many children.

One story stuck with me. A parent had eight children, but the boat could only take three, and Gadet's forces were closing in. He had to look his children in the face and decide which ones were troublesome and which ones were worth saving.

Another painful reality was how the crisis revealed the heartlessness of some businessmen, who charged almost $100 per passenger during that period. Imagine that. Even children who had lost their entire families along the way were expected to pay, and if they could not, they were left behind.

The night my girlfriend left with her family, Gadet's men arrived and killed people there. The mission was clear: kill as many people as possible.

The massacres in the churches, hospitals, and schools — there are still survivors who know these stories one by one.

My girlfriend was only 11 years old then. She never understood the full severity of what was happening. I remember her saying that as long as she had eaten, everything seemed fine. And that is how children think. She did not understand the stakes. But even now, I can hear fear in her voice when she talks about it, and it is not something any child should ever have to experience.

Everything about the Bor Massacre remains blurry because when history survives only through word of mouth, the world forgets. The Juba Massacre is relatively well documented in the book Bloody Nile, allegedly by Tim Edward. But the Bor Massacre remains trapped in the memories of 11-year-olds and government reports that focus only on their own roles rather than what the real victims endured.

We need to document our own stories. Otherwise, just like 1991, this too will be brushed away.

I hope this is also a reminder that this government is always ours to pay the price for. But when it is time to eat, the people who killed us and the people who provoked them sit down together and dine, while we rebuild from the ashes. The Nuer are not blind. They know who is Bor and who is Bahr el Ghazal. They know who is behind their suffering, but they know who is vulnerable and who is untouchable. Get your facts straight: you will always be collateral for this government. There is no middle ground. Whatever is cooking in Juba, you will be the one paying the price for it when the time comes, so put your inter-communal shit together and prepare, because it is only a matter of time before Juba erupts.

And I know some fool is going to scream "tribalism" in response to this. Shut the fuck up and go worship your godfather Riek Machar. For all I care, he can rot in hell.
