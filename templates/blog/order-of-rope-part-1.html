Order of Rope — Part 1

John GarangShort story · April 5, 2026 · 5 min read
johngarang.com

Sherkat was not marked on important maps, yet it existed long before roads were widened or promises were made. It was a wild forest that did not foresee a day it would be tamed to harbour a population whose governance closed its doors to.

Before the forest gave way to people, a couple had a son, gifted by the gods in a season of waiting. The elders remembered the year; the rains came quite late and left very early. Crops rose halfway and then stopped, as if reconsidering their purpose.

"Name him Deng," whispered his bedridden grandfather. "In a time of want, the ancestors forged fortune from soil and breathed life into it."

On his birth night, Deng carried weight, but the silence of infanthood carried more. The spiritual leaders had promised salvation through a son, but times were so tough that fortune-telling had lost its spark. His mother recalled he was no ordinary child; he learned patience before he could crawl. He grew up to watch more than question, listen more than he answered. The town loved him for this; perhaps it was an implicit way of projecting that they expected more action and little noise from him.

Before Deng's dreams saw the stirrings of manhood, he saw a man from afar, hung like a chandelier — grand in shape but without its light or beauty. He did not understand why people were wailing or whispering. No one mentioned the name at first; names arrived later, when the shock had settled.

When the incredulous crowd started to disperse, Deng moved a few steps closer — steps that sealed his fate. The man was bigger than his father, but there was no doubt that no townsman had ever worn a combination of clothes like the ones on him. It was not common for boys his age to handle such a sight, but he stood his ground, watching his father, quiet the way they both had always been.

The dry friction of fibre against bark and skin revealed to him that rope was more than a hanging line. His father was taken by time in a way ordinary men did not disappear. Some things must not be said of the departed, but there was moisture to signal a struggle. Not that he had wanted to live, but life refuses to leave when we no longer have the will. Governments take a lot from the common man and barely give a handful; it was beyond their mandate to touch the dead. They had to wait for the law while absorbing this ugly sight.

By the time the right people arrived to cut the body down, the sun was already high. Passersby went about their business; men adjusted their collars, women covered their mouths, and a common man rehearsed a prayer, for religion in this town did not permit clergy to pray for men who sought to see God before He called for them.

For every other man, this was just another day — sad, but life would move on. For Deng, his chest tightened, not exactly out of grief or fear, but because he knew this ending and had been rehearsing it quietly for years. On the day of his father's burial, old men spoke in measured tones. They told him to stand closer to the grave, to receive condolences meant for older men. His uncle remarked, "You are now the man of the house. Your father has left an unfinished sentence, and his shoes are still shaped like his feet. The shoes will not change shape."

Every male speaker lodged responsibility into his bones before he could accept the reality or refuse it. The women were softer and affectionate with their words. What came first were praises of his father.

"He was a good man," one elderly lady said.

"He kept to himself," said another.

They comforted his mother through it all, then comforted him, reminding him that many orphans had passed through this path and become chiefs and successful family men. One lady reminded him that grief must be swallowed like the bitter herbs traditional doctors prescribe — not because they are sweet, but because a bitter swallow keeps a dying soul alive a day longer.

Deng stood at the edge of the grave, hands in his pockets, gathering their words, all while trying to remember if he had ever asked his old man how life was. He could not.

Time passed as time must, and the years moved the way they do in such places — unevenly. Some felt rushed, others dragged, all dependent on the height and width of your granary. Deng grew thin, then tall, then quiet.

In Sherkat, men were seldom raised. Like a mechanic's tools, they were assembled slowly, with little care, so they may fit time's toolbox. They were not assembled with love, for it was an ancient belief that care breeds weakness, and life does not forgive the weak. They were built with warnings, because lessons scar permanently, and the economy does not allow them to inherit anything but silence.

A boy must learn early that his body is a container — one that quietly stores whatever falls into it. The town does not dine on soft accounts.

As success denotes, Deng went back to school all the way to university. He learned terminologies that sounded important. He studied politics, systems, and futures. He learned to write essays about his people, town, and nation. He mastered the accents and meanings of Arabic words that got men into big offices.

When he finished, he came home with his degree, his name printed firmly and beautifully on it. His mother cried. This was a landmark she and her late husband never reached, and somehow hoped their offspring would continue their legacy from where access limited them. The degree was placed in a frame, as if to signal the future.

The town changed clothes around him. New shops appeared, and old ones folded. The degree started collecting dust, the frame cracked, and Deng was no longer young enough to be forgiven for waiting for a breakthrough — yet chance respects neither age nor endurance.
